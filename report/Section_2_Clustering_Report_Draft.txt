2. CLUSTERING

After some experimentation with feature engineering, we decided to add three new features, specified as follows:
a) listings_per_day = n_listings / max(n_listing_days, 1)
b) annual_mileage = mileage_median / (vehicle_age_median + 1)
c) adverse_history_index = pct_isCab + pct_salvage + pct_theft_title

Together with four original variables, we selected seven numerical features (price_median, pct_is_new, horsepower_median, city_fuel_economy_median, listings_per_day, annual_mileage, and adverse_history_index) from the 22 numerical columns. These features were chosen because they represent the core dimensions of the dealership business, whereas the remaining features were dropped due to strong redundancy with selected ones—such as highway_fuel_economy_median (with city_fuel_economy_median), engine_displacement_median (with horsepower_median), vehicle body dimensions, and raw variables already combined into the three engineered features.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Using the transformed, Min–Max scaled dataset from Section 1.3.3 (with the same transformation and scaling pipeline applied to the three engineered features), we applied K-Means (k-means++ initialization, 20 runs, random_state = 42) to all 13,843 sellers on both the 22-feature baseline and the 7-feature selected dataset. Comparing Tables 2.1 and 2.2 (and Figure 2.1b), using the 7-feature selected dataset clearly outperforms the 22-feature baseline across all k, raising the Silhouette score at k = 4 by +62.2% (0.246 to 0.399) and forming much tighter clusters once redundant features are removed. On the selected dataset (Figure 2.1a–b), k = 2 achieves the highest Silhouette (0.578), but it only divides sellers into two broad groups (new-car vs. used-car dealers) and leaves SSE high (1,244.6). Moving from k = 2 to k = 4 brings the steepest SSE reductions (down to 755.2), whereas beyond k = 4 the elbow curve begins to flatten and Silhouette drops below 0.35 (0.348 at k = 5). We therefore select k = 4 (Sil = 0.399, SSE = 755.2) as the elbow point that balances cluster compactness with a detailed segmentation of dealership inventories.

As shown by the centroid parallel coordinates (Figure 2.2a) and real-unit cluster medians (Figure 2.2b), the four clusters at k = 4 separate clearly across the first five features while converging closely on horsepower_median and city_fuel_economy_median. Specifically, C1 (3,914 sellers, 28.3%) captures large new-car dealers with the highest listing activity (median 2.59 listings_per_day), high prices ($30,093), the highest new-vehicle share (68%), and near-zero annual_mileage (9 miles/year). C3 (2,054 sellers, 14.8%) represents mixed new and late-model dealers that maintain similarly high prices ($28,772) but have a lower new-vehicle share (45%) and higher annual_mileage (6,661 miles/year). C2, the largest group (6,478 sellers, 46.8%), corresponds to standard used-car lots with the lowest listing rate (1.40 listings_per_day), the lowest prices ($12,999), 0% new cars, and high annual_mileage (11,059 miles/year). Finally, C0 (1,397 sellers, 10.1%) isolates used-car sellers distinguished by a sharp peak in adverse_history_index (0.40, compared with 0.10–0.14 in the other groups), reflecting inventories with a high share of former cab, salvage, or theft-title vehicles.

2.2 Density-Based Clustering
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Unlike centroid-based methods that force every dealership into a spherical partition, density-based clustering identifies dense regions of arbitrary shape while isolating sparse transition points and outliers as noise. In high-dimensional spaces such as the full 22-feature dataset (d = 22), pairwise Euclidean distances become less discriminative due to feature redundancy and multiple zero-inflated indicators; preliminary checks on the 22-feature space confirm that DBSCAN collapses nearly all dealerships into a single macro-cluster plus peripheral noise. We therefore focus the density-based analysis directly on the selected seven-feature Min–Max space (d = 7), where local neighborhood densities remain meaningful and comparable across features.

To determine the neighborhood parameters MinPts and epsilon (eps), we follow the standard dimensional heuristic MinPts >= d + 1 = 8, using 2d = 14 as a reference starting point and evaluating candidate values k = MinPts in {8, 14, 16, 20, 30}. For each candidate, we plot the sorted k-th nearest neighbor (k-NN) distances. Because automatic knee detection (KneeLocator, S = 1.0) captures the extreme upper-tail bend caused by sparse boundary outliers (eps = 0.176 to 0.266, where over 95% of sellers merge into a single cluster), we examine the lower onset of steep curvature at roughly half that distance (eps/2 ~ 0.088 to 0.133, Table 2.2a), which marks the actual density transition between dense seller cores and sparser transition regions.

Table 2.2a: Reference k-NN distance knees (eps) and lower transition thresholds (eps/2) across candidate MinPts values (d = 7)
| Parameter | MinPts = 8 | MinPts = 14 | MinPts = 16 | MinPts = 20 | MinPts = 30 |
| :--- | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| Upper-tail knee (eps) | 0.1757 | 0.1937 | 0.2177 | 0.2182 | 0.2661 |
| Lower transition (eps/2) | 0.0878 | 0.0968 | 0.1088 | 0.1091 | 0.1330 |

Testing the transition region around eps = 0.10 across MinPts values shows that smaller neighborhoods (MinPts in 8 to 16) fragment the inventory into 4 to 10 overly narrow micro-clusters, whereas MinPts between 18 and 22 stabilizes the partition into three coherent density cores. Fixing MinPts = 20 as the representative midpoint, we evaluate eps over [0.06, 0.20] under three practical selection criteria: at least two clusters, an unassigned noise share below 20%, and a minimum cluster size of at least 50 dealerships (Table 2.3).

Table 2.3: DBSCAN sensitivity across candidate radius values (eps) at MinPts = 20, alongside HDBSCAN on the selected seven-feature dataset (d = 7)
| Method & Parameters | Clusters | Cluster sizes | Noise (%) | Sil. (assigned) |
| :--- | :---: | :--- | :---: | :---: |
| DBSCAN (eps = 0.090, MinPts = 20) | 5 | [7013, 2302, 1064, 86, 68] | 23.9% | 0.397 |
| DBSCAN (eps = 0.100, MinPts = 20) (selected) | 3 | [8568, 2926, 75] | 16.4% | 0.550 |
| DBSCAN (eps = 0.115, MinPts = 20) | 2 | [9173, 3468] | 8.7% | 0.618 |
| HDBSCAN (min_cluster_size = 100, min_samples = 20) | 3 | [6560, 2662, 669] | 28.6% | 0.521 |

As summarized in Table 2.3, reducing the radius to eps = 0.090 pushes the unassigned noise rate to 23.9% (3,310 sellers) and splits the data into five uneven groups with a lower assigned-seller Silhouette of 0.397. Conversely, expanding the radius to eps = 0.115 increases the Silhouette score to 0.618 and reduces noise to 8.7%, but merges smaller specialized dealerships into two broad macro-clusters. We therefore select eps = 0.100 with MinPts = 20 as our primary density-based configuration, achieving a strong assigned-seller Silhouette of 0.550 while keeping noise at 16.4% (2,274 sellers) and preserving three interpretable seller segments.

Examining the real-unit medians across the seven selected features reveals clear operational profiles for the three discovered clusters and the unassigned noise group:
- Cluster 0 — Standard Used-Car Core (8,568 sellers, 61.9%): Represents the dominant dense core of traditional pre-owned dealerships (pct_is_new = 0.00), characterized by moderate inventory turnover (listings_per_day = 1.49), accessible pricing (price_median = $14,999), established vehicle usage (annual_mileage = 10,835 miles/year), low adverse history (adverse_history_index = 0.12), and mainstream engine specs (horsepower_median = 240 HP, city_fuel_economy_median = 20.0 MPG).
- Cluster 1 — New-Car Oriented Inventory (2,926 sellers, 21.1%): Captures high-volume franchise dealerships (listings_per_day = 2.60) focused predominantly on brand-new vehicles (pct_is_new = 0.68, annual_mileage = 9 miles/year) at higher price points (price_median = $29,705), with clean histories (adverse_history_index = 0.11) and slightly higher power and fuel efficiency (horsepower_median = 245 HP, city_fuel_economy_median = 21.5 MPG).
- Cluster 2 — High-Adverse / Commercial Fleet Used Niche (75 sellers, 0.5%): Isolates a tight, high-density pocket of fast-moving commercial and fleet resellers (listings_per_day = 4.08, pct_is_new = 0.00, price_median = $19,497, annual_mileage = 16,994 miles/year) whose inventories consist almost entirely of prior taxi/cab or adverse-history vehicles (adverse_history_index = 0.99, horsepower_median = 203 HP, city_fuel_economy_median = 24.0 MPG).
- Unassigned / Noise Sellers (2,274 sellers, 16.4%): Rather than pure data errors, these unassigned dealerships (listings_per_day = 2.32, price_median = $29,365, annual_mileage = 870 miles/year, pct_is_new = 0.57, adverse_history_index = 0.20, horsepower_median = 252 HP, city_fuel_economy_median = 21.0 MPG) predominantly represent hybrid new/used or certified pre-owned (CPO) operations that lie in sparser transition zones between the pure used-car core (Cluster 0) and the franchise new-car core (Cluster 1), alongside extreme high-price luxury outliers.

Finally, to evaluate whether hierarchical density thresholds can better accommodate varying local densities without a fixed global radius, we also tested HDBSCAN (min_cluster_size = 100, min_samples = 20) (Table 2.3). While HDBSCAN similarly recovers three core groups (sizes 6,560, 2,662, and 669; assigned Silhouette = 0.521), its stricter stability requirement leaves 28.6% of dealerships (3,952 sellers) unassigned, confirming that fixed-radius DBSCAN (eps = 0.100, MinPts = 20) provides a better balance between cluster separation and seller coverage on this dataset.

2.3 Hierarchical Clustering
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Agglomerative clustering was evaluated across four linkage criteria—Ward, Group Average, Max (Complete), and Min (Single)—using Euclidean (L2) distance on the 22-feature baseline and the 7-feature selected dataset (Scenario 3B, Section 2.1). Each hierarchy was cut at k = 4 to align with the K-Means partition (Tables 2.4–2.5), and the four Euclidean linkages on the selected dataset were visualized via truncated dendrograms of the last p = 15 merged branches (Figure 2.7).

| Linkage | Cluster sizes | Sil. | SSE |
| :--- | :--- | :---: | :---: |

Using the 7-feature selected dataset clearly outperforms the 22-feature baseline, raising Ward Silhouette by +48.8% (0.297 to 0.442) and Max Silhouette from 0.274 to 0.422. Within this space, Ward linkage (Figure 2.7a) gives the best overall result, forming four well-balanced clusters with clear upper splits and the lowest error (SSE = 794.4)—close to K-Means (SSE = 755.2) while achieving a higher Silhouette score (0.442 vs. 0.399). Although Group Average (Figure 2.7c) records the highest Silhouette (0.498), it divides the data poorly and leaves SSE high (1,239.4) because it keeps almost all sellers in two large groups and separates only 47 outliers into two tiny clusters. For the remaining two methods, Max linkage (Figure 2.7b) produces a more usable partition (Sil = 0.422, SSE = 1,011.4), though it splits large groups unevenly—leaving one small 90-seller cluster—and still has higher error than Ward. Finally, Min linkage (Figure 2.7d) fails due to the chaining effect and outlier sensitivity, linking 13,840 sellers into one giant cluster while leaving only three isolated points (Sil = 0.275, SSE = 3,465.5).

2.4 Final discussion
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Corresponding figure:
- fig2_8_final_evaluation_compact.png (synced copy: fig2_8_final_algorithm_synthesis.png)

[Option 1 — With Figure 2.8]
Across all clustering families, reducing the feature space from all 22 numerical variables to the seven selected features (Scenario 3B, Section 2.1) consistently improved clustering quality—raising the k = 4 Silhouette score from 0.246 to 0.399 for K-Means and from 0.297 to 0.442 for Ward linkage. To choose the best overall algorithm in this seven-feature Min–Max space, we compared the main methods across Silhouette separation, SSE, cluster-size balance, and seller coverage (Figure 2.8). All Silhouette and SSE comparisons use Euclidean distance on the evaluated sellers; for density-based methods, Silhouette excludes unassigned noise points (labels != -1), so a higher score does not represent the full 13,843-seller population.

At the selected four-group resolution (k = 4), Ward (Sil = 0.442, SSE = 794.4) and K-Means (Sil = 0.399, SSE = 755.2) provide the strongest overall descriptions, assigning 100% of sellers into four well-sized, interpretable dealership profiles ([7115, 4004, 2234, 490] in Ward and [6478, 3914, 2054, 1397] in K-Means). Ward achieves higher Silhouette separation while keeping its smallest group at a meaningful size (490 sellers, or 3.5%), whereas K-Means achieves the lowest overall error (SSE = 755.2) and the most balanced cluster sizes. By contrast, other Euclidean linkages illustrate why Silhouette alone cannot select a good partition: Max (Complete) scores Sil = 0.422 (SSE = 1,011.4) while leaving only 90 sellers in its smallest group, and Average reaches Sil = 0.498 (SSE = 1,239.4) only by splitting off 38 and 9 outliers into two tiny clusters.

Density-based methods show a similar trade-off between local cohesion and coverage. DBSCAN (eps = 0.10, MinPts = 20) finds three clusters ([8568, 2926, 75]) with a high assigned-seller Silhouette of 0.550, but leaves 16.4% of sellers unassigned as noise; HDBSCAN (min_cluster_size = 100, min_samples = 20) detects three denser cores ([6560, 2662, 669], Sil = 0.521) while classifying 28.6% of sellers as noise. These methods are useful for identifying dense regions, but unassigned points cannot automatically be treated as a separate business category. Overall, Ward and K-Means at k = 4 provide the most balanced full-coverage segmentation, distinguishing dealers primarily by new-vehicle share, price, mileage, and adverse history; reducing to two clusters yields a simpler broad summary, whereas five clusters fragment the main segments (lowering Ward Silhouette to 0.315). Rather than claiming a single ground-truth partition, these clusters summarize inventory structure in the selected feature space and remain distinct from seller-rating prediction, which is addressed next via supervised classification.
