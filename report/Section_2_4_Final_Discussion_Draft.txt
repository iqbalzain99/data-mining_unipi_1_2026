For the selected four-group level, Ward is our preferred description: it improves separation over K-Means, assigns every seller and avoids the tiny residual groups found with Average and Single linkage. Complete–Manhattan has a slightly higher Silhouette, but its 101-seller group is less useful for the intended broad profiling than Ward's smallest group of 490 sellers. The preference is therefore based on separation, group sizes, coverage and interpretability together, not on winning every metric. K-Means remains a useful reference; the additional Bisecting and GMM results do not provide a stronger separation score than Ward at this resolution.

Figure 2.4a compares separation, noise coverage and group sizes. In particular, DBSCAN's higher assigned-seller Silhouette cannot be interpreted as superiority over full-coverage Ward. The notebook additionally evaluates K-Means and Ward on exactly the same sellers retained by each density method; these are coverage-controlled descriptive comparisons, not test-set performance.

Figure 2.4b shows every seller in an age-adjusted-mileage versus price view for K-Means, Ward and DBSCAN. The displayed axes use symmetric-log scaling to retain zero values and the full range. Groups can overlap in this projection because fitting uses seven features. Colours identify groups within each panel only; grey denotes DBSCAN noise.

Overall, the data support a broad inventory-related separation and a more detailed four-group description. Neither the ratios nor the clustering justify labels such as CPO lease returns or premium business specialists. Franchise status and new/used composition help describe the groups, but share information with the inputs and are not independent ground truth. Finally, these clusters are not seller-rating classes: predicting the rating category remains a separate classification task.
